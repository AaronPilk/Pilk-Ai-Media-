No-code tools have become remarkably capable. You can build a customer portal, an internal dashboard or a simple mobile app without a developer. So when does it make sense to pay for custom development? Here is an honest comparison from a studio that builds custom apps but often recommends no-code first.

## What is no-code development?

No-code platforms let you build software with visual editors, templates and drag-and-drop logic instead of code. Common categories include website builders, app builders, database tools, automation platforms and internal tool builders. Low-code platforms sit in between, allowing some code for custom pieces.

## What is custom development?

Custom development means writing software for your specific needs: a mobile app for iPhone and Android, a web app, a backend with your data and logic, and integrations with your systems. You or your developer control the code.

## How do no-code and custom compare?

| | No-code | Custom |
|---|---|---|
| Up-front cost | Low | Higher |
| Speed to first version | Days to weeks | Weeks to months |
| Flexibility | Limited by the platform | Anything you can specify |
| Performance | Fine for small scale | Tuned to your needs |
| Ownership | You rent the platform | You own the code you paid for |
| Ongoing cost | Subscriptions that grow with users | Hosting, maintenance and updates |
| Vendor risk | Pricing or feature changes | Lower, but you maintain it |

## When does no-code make sense?

- **Testing an idea** before investing heavily.
- **Internal tools** for a small team: dashboards, trackers, simple workflows.
- **Simple customer-facing tools**: booking, forms, basic portals.
- **Tight budgets** where speed matters more than polish.
- **Processes that change often**, where editing yourself is valuable.

## When does custom development make sense?

- **The app is the product** you sell or a core part of your service.
- **You need native mobile features**: push notifications, camera, location, offline use or Bluetooth.
- **Performance and scale matter**: many users, heavy data or real-time features.
- **Design and experience must stand out.**
- **No-code costs grow** with users until custom is cheaper over time.
- **Security, compliance or data control** require full ownership.

## What are the hidden costs of no-code?

- Per-user or usage pricing that climbs as you grow.
- Workarounds for features the platform does not support.
- Difficulty moving data or logic elsewhere later.
- Platform changes outside your control.

None of these rule out no-code; they are reasons to plan for growth.

## What are the hidden costs of custom development?

- Ongoing maintenance and updates, including yearly mobile operating system changes.
- Hosting and third-party service fees.
- The need for a developer to make changes.

## How do you move from no-code to custom?

Treat the no-code version as a prototype that proves what users want. Before rebuilding:

1. List the features people actually use and the ones they ignore.
2. Export your data and document your workflows.
3. Build the custom version around proven features first.
4. Run both briefly while users switch.

## How do you decide for your project?

Ask: Is this the product or a supporting tool? Do we need native mobile features? How many users in two years? How often will it change? If it is a supporting tool with few users that changes often, start no-code. If it is the product with native needs and growth ahead, plan custom. See also [app vs website](/blog/app-vs-website/).

## What does a realistic path from no-code to custom look like?

Picture a cleaning company that wants customers to book, pay and manage recurring visits. It starts with a booking tool and a payment form, plus a simple no-code customer portal, for a small monthly cost. Within a year, hundreds of customers use it every week, the team keeps hitting limits on scheduling rules and notifications, and per-user fees keep climbing. At that point a custom app makes sense: the business knows exactly which features customers use, has real usage data, and can justify the investment. The no-code version did its job by proving demand cheaply.

## Who should maintain the app after launch?

With no-code, someone on your team can usually make changes. With custom development, plan for a developer or studio on a support plan to handle updates, fixes and new features. Agree on response times and costs before launch.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can no-code apps go on the App Store?

Some no-code platforms can publish to the App Store and Google Play. Check the platform's capabilities and whether the result meets Apple's and Google's review guidelines.

### Is no-code cheaper than custom development?

Usually at the start. As users and features grow, subscriptions and workarounds can make custom cheaper over time.

### Can I own a no-code app?

You own your content and data, but the app runs on the platform. Moving it elsewhere usually means rebuilding.

### Should a startup start with no-code?

Often yes, to prove demand quickly. Plan to rebuild custom if the product takes off or hits platform limits.
