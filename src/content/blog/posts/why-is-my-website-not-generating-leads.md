When an owner tells us "the website doesn't bring in anything," the fix is rarely a full redesign. Usually one or two specific things are broken, and they are cheaper to fix than a new site. Here are the nine causes we see most, in the order we check them, so you can find yours before spending money.

## Is anyone actually visiting your website?

Start here, because nothing else matters if the answer is no. Open Google Search Console (free) and look at the Performance report: how many people saw your site in search (impressions) and how many clicked. If you have never set it up, our guide to [Google Search Console for beginners](/blog/google-search-console-for-beginners/) walks through it.

If you get almost no impressions, you have a visibility problem, not a website problem. Common reasons:

- The site is new and has not been indexed yet.
- Pages do not target what customers actually search for.
- There is no Google Business Profile, or it is incomplete.
- A setting is blocking search engines, such as a leftover "noindex" tag from development.

## Are you attracting the right visitors?

Traffic is not the same as buyers. A blog post that ranks for a broad question may bring thousands of visitors who will never hire you, while a service page for "[service] in [your city]" brings a few dozen who might. In Search Console, look at which search terms bring clicks. If they are informational or far from what you sell, you need pages built around the searches your buyers type.

For local businesses, those buyers usually search with a city or "near me," which is why a complete Business Profile and a page for each service area matter. Our [local SEO checklist](/blog/local-seo-checklist-for-small-businesses/) covers the basics.

## Does your homepage say what you do in five seconds?

A visitor should know three things without scrolling: what you do, who it is for and where you work. "Excellence in Service Since 1998" tells them none of those. "Licensed roof repair and replacement in St. Petersburg and Pinellas County" tells them all three.

Read your first screen out loud as if you were a stranger. If it could describe any business, rewrite it.

## Is the next step obvious on every page?

Many sites hide the call to action. Every page should have one clear next step that matches what makes you money: call, book, request a quote or fill out a form. Put it at the top, repeat it after key sections, and make the phone number tappable on phones.

Avoid offering five equal options. One primary action and one backup (usually a phone number) works better than a menu of choices.

## Is your contact form too long or broken?

Two problems are common here:

1. **The form asks for too much.** Every extra required field costs you some people. Ask only for what you need to respond. We go deeper in [lead form best practices](/blog/lead-form-best-practices/).
2. **The form does not work.** We regularly find forms that send to an old employee's inbox, land in spam, or fail silently. Submit your own form today and see where it goes and how long it takes.

## Does the site load fast and work on a phone?

Most local searches happen on phones. If your site takes a long time to load, or the menu and buttons are hard to tap, people leave before they read anything. Test it on your own phone over cellular, not office Wi-Fi. Google's free PageSpeed Insights tool shows what is slowing it down.

## Do visitors trust you enough to contact you?

People compare two or three businesses before reaching out. Give them reasons to pick you:

- Real photos of your team and work, not stock images.
- Reviews, ideally pulled from Google so they are verifiable.
- Licences, insurance or certifications if your trade has them.
- Clear pricing or at least starting prices. Hiding all pricing makes many people move on.
- A real person's name on the about page.

## Are leads coming in but getting lost?

This is the most expensive problem because you already paid to attract the lead. Ask yourself:

- How long does it take for someone to reply to a form, on average?
- What happens to calls after hours?
- Does every lead land in one place, or across inboxes, texts and voicemail?

Fast replies win. A [Harvard Business Review study](https://hbr.org/2011/03/the-short-life-of-online-sales-leads) found that companies contacting leads within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify them as those that waited longer. We explain the research and the fix in [speed to lead](/blog/speed-to-lead/). An automatic text reply and a simple CRM fix this for most small businesses.

## Do you have a way to measure what is working?

Without tracking, every change is a guess. At minimum, set up:

- **Search Console** to see search visibility.
- **Analytics** to see which pages people visit.
- **Form and call tracking** so you know which pages and sources produce leads.

Then change one thing at a time and watch the numbers for a few weeks.

## Should you fix your current site or rebuild it?

Fix first if the problems are content, calls to action, forms or follow-up. Those are cheaper than a rebuild and often enough. Rebuild when the site is slow at its core, hard to edit, impossible to use on a phone, or built on a platform you do not control.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many visitors does a website need to get leads?

There is no fixed number, because it depends on how well the visitors match what you sell. A local service site with a few hundred well-targeted visitors a month can produce steady leads, while thousands of off-target visitors may produce none.

### Why do people visit my website but not call?

Usually because the next step is unclear, the site does not build enough trust, or the phone number is hard to find or tap on mobile. Check your first screen, your calls to action and your reviews.

### Can a new website fix low leads?

Only if the old site was the problem. If nobody is finding you, a nicer site will not change that. If people visit but do not act, or leads are not followed up, a rebuild can help, but fixing those specific issues is often cheaper.

### How do I know if my contact form is working?

Submit it yourself from a phone and from a computer, then check where the message lands and how long it takes. Do this every few months and after any website change.
