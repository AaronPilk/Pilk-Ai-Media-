Price is the first question almost every owner asks us, and the honest answer is that "a website" covers everything from a one-page profile to a lead machine wired into a CRM. This guide breaks the cost into the parts you are actually paying for, shows our own starting prices, and explains when the cheap option is the right one.

## What are you actually paying for when you buy a website?

Every website bill, from a builder subscription to an agency invoice, pays for the same handful of things. The difference in price comes from who does each one and how much of it you need.

- **The domain**: your address, like yourbusiness.com, renewed every year.
- **Hosting**: the servers that deliver the site to visitors, paid monthly or yearly.
- **Design**: how the site looks and how the pages are laid out.
- **Build**: turning the design into pages that load fast and work on phones.
- **Content**: the words, photos and service details. Owners often forget this is work.
- **Lead capture**: forms, booking, call buttons and where those leads end up.
- **Upkeep**: edits, security updates, backups and fixes after launch.

When you do all of these yourself, you pay mostly in time. When you hire someone, you pay for their time and judgment instead. Neither is wrong; it depends on which you have more of.

## How much does a DIY website builder cost?

Website builders like Wix, Squarespace and GoDaddy charge a monthly or yearly subscription, and the plan you need depends on whether you want online booking, payments or more storage. Check each builder's current pricing page, because plans and promotions change often.

The subscription is the visible cost. The hidden cost is your hours: writing every page, choosing and cropping photos, connecting the domain, setting up forms, and fixing things when a template update moves your layout. For a brand-new business with more time than money, that trade is often worth it, and we say so in our guide on [whether a web design agency is worth it](/is-a-web-design-agency-worth-it/).

## What does a website cost every month after launch?

The build is a one-time cost, but a site keeps costing a little every month. Plan for:

- **Domain renewal** each year.
- **Hosting**, which you can manage yourself or hand off. Our hosting and support plans start at {{price:hosting}} and cover hosting, monitoring, backups and ongoing edits, month to month.
- **Software you connect**, such as a CRM, booking tool or email service, each with its own subscription.
- **Your time**, if you make your own edits.

A cheap build with no plan for upkeep tends to get expensive later, usually when something breaks the week you are busiest.

## Why do website quotes vary so much between companies?

You can get three quotes for "the same website" that differ by thousands of dollars, and usually they are not quoting the same thing. The most common differences:

1. **Who writes the content.** Some quotes assume you supply every word and photo; others include writing.
2. **How many pages.** A five-page site and a twenty-page site with a page for every service are different jobs.
3. **Lead handling.** A contact form that emails you is simple. Forms that feed a CRM, text the lead back and book a call are not.
4. **Ownership.** Some cheap offers rent you a site you never own. Ask who owns the domain, the design and the code. We cover this in [who owns my website](/blog/who-owns-my-website/).
5. **What happens after launch.** Edits, hosting and support are sometimes included, sometimes extra.

When you compare quotes, line them up against that list. The cheaper one often leaves out the part that brings in customers.

## When is a cheap website the right call?

Spending less is the smart move in a few situations:

- You just started and do not yet know which services will sell.
- Most of your customers come from referrals, and the site only needs to confirm you are real.
- You need a simple page with your hours, phone number and location.
- You have the time and enjoy doing it yourself.

Spending more makes sense when the website is supposed to bring in work: when you run ads to it, when you compete with businesses that rank above you, or when leads slip through because nobody follows up fast enough. In those cases the extra cost is paying for the parts a builder will not set up for you. If your current site gets traffic but no calls, read [why a website does not generate leads](/blog/why-is-my-website-not-generating-leads/) before you rebuild anything.

## How do you get an accurate quote for your website?

You will get a closer number, faster, if you go in with answers to these questions:

1. What should a visitor do on the site: call, book, fill out a form or buy?
2. How many services or locations need their own page?
3. Do you have your logo, photos and text, or do you need help with them?
4. Which tools need to connect: a CRM, calendar, payment system or ads account?
5. Who will make changes after launch?

Mostly to buy back time and get the parts that turn visitors into leads: page structure, fast load times, tracking and follow-up. If you have more time than budget and only need a simple page, a builder is a reasonable choice.
