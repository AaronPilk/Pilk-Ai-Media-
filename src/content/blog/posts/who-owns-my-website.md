The worst website stories we hear are not about ugly designs. They are about owners who cannot get into their own site, cannot move it, or find out their domain is registered to a developer who stopped answering emails. Ownership is easy to get right at the start and painful to fix later. Here is what you should own, how to check, and what to do if you are already stuck.

## What parts of a website can you own?

A "website" is really several separate things, and each one can belong to a different person:

- **The domain name** (yourbusiness.com), held at a registrar like GoDaddy, Namecheap or Cloudflare.
- **The hosting account**, where the site's files live and are served from.
- **The design and code**: the layouts, templates and programming that make the site work.
- **The content**: your words, photos, logo, reviews and data.
- **Connected accounts**: Google Business Profile, Search Console, analytics, the CRM, ad accounts and email.

You can own all of these, rent some of them, or lose track of who controls which. The goal is to control the ones you would need to keep the business running if your designer disappeared tomorrow.

## Who owns the domain name?

Whoever is listed as the registrant at the registrar. That should be you or your business, with your email on the account and your login.

This is the single most important check. If a developer registers your domain in their name or their account, they control where your website and often your email point. Even a well-meaning developer who gets sick or closes shop can leave you locked out. If someone else already holds your domain, ask them to transfer it to an account in your name now, while the relationship is good.

## Does a web designer own the website they build for you?

It depends on the contract. By default, under US copyright law, the person who creates a design or code generally owns it unless a written agreement transfers it or it is made by an employee as part of their job (the US Copyright Office explains this in its circular on [works made for hire](https://www.copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf)). That is why the contract matters more than the invoice.

A fair agreement usually says something like: once the project is paid in full, the work made specifically for you is yours, and anything reusable the designer brings along (their templates, code libraries, design systems) is licensed to you permanently so you can keep using, editing and moving the site.

That is how our own terms work. Your content always stays yours, the custom work transfers to you once the project is paid in full, and our reusable materials come with a permanent licence that does not depend on keeping a hosting plan with us. ## Do you own a website built on Wix, Squarespace or a builder platform?

You own your content, and you can usually take your domain with you. You generally do not own the site itself in a form you can move. The design and code are part of the platform, so leaving usually means rebuilding somewhere else and copying your content over by hand.

That is not a reason to avoid builders. It is a trade-off to understand. If you might want to move later, keep copies of your text and photos outside the builder, and make sure the domain is registered to you rather than bundled inside the builder account where it is hard to transfer.

## What are the red flags in a website contract?

Watch for these before you sign:

1. **The domain will be registered "for you"** with no mention of whose name and account it sits in.
2. **The monthly fee is what keeps your site alive**, and stopping it means losing the site, not just the hosting service.
3. **No mention of ownership at all.** Silence usually favours whoever created the work.
4. **No admin access for you**, only "send us any changes."
5. **Cancellation fees or "buyout" prices** to get your own files.
6. **Accounts created under the agency's name** for things that belong to you, like your Google Business Profile or ad account.

A hosting and support plan is normal and useful. What matters is that it pays for hosting and upkeep, not for the right to keep your site.

## What should you ask a web designer about ownership?

Ask these five questions and get the answers in writing:

1. Whose name and account will the domain be registered under?
2. Once I have paid in full, do I own the finished website?
3. If I stop paying for hosting or support, can I take the site with me, and how?
4. Will I have admin access to the site, hosting, analytics and Search Console?
5. Which accounts will be created in my name, and which in yours?

## What if your developer will not give you access?

Start with a calm written request listing exactly what you need: domain transfer, hosting logins, site files or export. Most disputes end there. If the domain is in their name, your registrar's support team and the ICANN transfer rules are the next stop. If you have a contract that says the work is yours, that document is your strongest tool.

Then fix the root problem. Register a new domain in your name if you must, set up your own Google and analytics accounts, and make sure the next build is set up in accounts you control. ## Frequently asked questions

### Can a web designer hold my website hostage?

They can make leaving very hard if they control your domain or hosting and your contract does not say the site is yours. Registering the domain in your own name and getting ownership in writing prevents almost every version of this.

### Do I own my website if I stop paying for hosting?

It depends on the agreement. With an owned site, stopping hosting means you move the files elsewhere and keep running. With a rented or platform site, stopping usually means the site goes offline.

### Should my email be on the same account as my website?

Your email should sit in an account you control, such as Google Workspace or Microsoft 365, linked to a domain registered in your name. That way a change of web designer never touches your email.

### Who owns the photos on my website?

You own photos you took or paid to have taken under a contract that gives you the rights. Stock photos and MLS listing photos come with licences that limit how you can use them, so check the terms before reusing them elsewhere.
