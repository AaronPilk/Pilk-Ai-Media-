Owners often expect a redesign to cost a fraction of a new website because "the site already exists." Sometimes it does cost less. Often it does not, and the reasons are worth knowing before you get quotes. This guide covers what you are paying for in a redesign, what moves the price, and how to avoid the most expensive mistake: losing the search traffic you already earned.

## Is a redesign cheaper than building a new website?

Rarely by much. Most redesigns are effectively new builds: new design, new code, new page structure. What you save is content. If your service descriptions, photos and reviews are still accurate, the project moves faster and needs less writing.

What you sometimes pay extra for is protecting what the old site has: search rankings, links from other sites, and pages people have bookmarked. That work, mapping old addresses to new ones and setting redirects, does not exist on a brand-new site.

## What makes a redesign cost more?

These are the factors that push a redesign quote up:

1. **Lots of pages.** A 60-page site with a page per service and location takes longer to rebuild and redirect than a 6-page one.
2. **Outdated content.** If most of the text needs rewriting, you are paying for copywriting, not just design.
3. **Moving platforms.** Leaving a builder like Wix for an owned site means rebuilding everything, not exporting it.
4. **Integrations.** Booking, payments, CRM, IDX listings for real estate or calculators for mortgage sites each add work.
5. **A blog or resource library to migrate.** Every post needs moving, checking and redirecting.
6. **Compliance review.** Mortgage, title and other regulated businesses need disclosures and licence details checked.

## How do you redesign a website without losing Google rankings?

This is where cheap redesigns go wrong. When page addresses change and nobody sets redirects, Google sees the old pages disappear and the new ones as strangers. Traffic can drop sharply and take months to recover.

Protect your rankings with these steps:

- **Export every current URL** from your sitemap or Search Console before anything changes.
- **Keep the same URLs** where possible. Change design, not addresses.
- **Map every old URL to its new home** and set permanent (301) redirects for anything that moves.
- **Keep page titles and key content** on the pages that already rank, improving rather than replacing them.
- **Submit the new sitemap** in Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools on launch day.
- **Watch Search Console** for errors in the weeks after launch and fix broken links quickly.

## When does a redesign make sense, and when is it a waste?

Redesign when:

- The site is slow, hard to use on a phone, or impossible for you to edit.
- Your services, brand or prices have changed and the site no longer matches.
- You are about to run ads and the landing pages would waste the budget.
- You do not control the platform or hosting and want to own your site.

Do not redesign just because the site "feels old" if it is fast, ranks well and brings in leads. Fix what is broken instead. If leads are the problem, read [why a website does not generate leads](/blog/why-is-my-website-not-generating-leads/) first. You may need better follow-up, not a new homepage.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will a redesign hurt my SEO?

It can if URLs change without redirects or ranking content is removed. Done carefully, with redirects, kept titles and a resubmitted sitemap, a redesign usually holds rankings and often improves them through faster pages.

### Can I redesign just part of my website?

Yes. Some businesses start with the homepage and main service pages, then update the rest later. It costs less up front, though the site may look inconsistent until the work is finished.

### How often should a small business redesign its website?

There is no fixed schedule. Many businesses redesign every few years when the site becomes slow, hard to edit or no longer reflects the business. Regular small updates reduce the need for big redesigns.

### Is it cheaper to move from Wix to an owned website?

The move itself is a rebuild, so it costs about the same as a new site. The long-term benefit is owning the site and not paying a platform subscription for a design you cannot take with you.
