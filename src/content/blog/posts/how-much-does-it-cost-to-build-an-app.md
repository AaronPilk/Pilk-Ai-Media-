App quotes range so widely that they can seem made up. One company says a few thousand dollars, another says a few hundred thousand, for what sounds like the same idea. The difference is almost always scope: how many features, how many platforms and how much needs to happen behind the scenes. This guide explains what you are paying for, what drives the price and how to keep the first version affordable.

## What are you paying for when you build an app?

An app has more moving parts than a website. A typical build includes:

- **Product planning**: defining exactly what the first version does and does not do.
- **Design**: screens, flows and how the app feels to use.
- **The app itself**: the code that runs on iPhone and Android.
- **The backend**: servers, database, accounts, login and anything that stores data.
- **Integrations**: payments, maps, messaging, calendars, AI models or your existing systems.
- **Admin tools**: a dashboard for you to manage users and content.
- **Testing**: on real devices, across screen sizes and operating system versions.
- **App store submission**: listings, screenshots, privacy details and getting through review.

Cheap quotes usually leave out the backend, admin tools or app store work, which you discover later.

## What drives the cost of an app up or down?

| Factor | Lower cost | Higher cost |
|---|---|---|
| Features | One core job done well | Many features at once |
| Platforms | One platform, or a cross-platform build | Separate native apps for iOS and Android |
| Accounts and data | No login or simple accounts | Roles, permissions, sensitive data |
| Integrations | None or standard ones | Custom systems, real-time data, AI |
| Design | Proven patterns | Highly custom interactions |
| Compliance | Standard privacy | Health, finance or other regulated data |

## What does an app cost after it launches?

Budget for ongoing costs from day one:

- **App store accounts**: Apple's Developer Program is $99 a year, and Google Play charges a one-time $25 registration fee.
- **Hosting and backend services**, which grow with users.
- **Third-party services**: maps, messaging, email, payments and AI usage, often billed per use.
- **Updates**: Apple and Google release new operating system versions every year, and apps need updates to keep working well and stay in the stores.
- **Support and fixes** as real users find issues.

A useful rule of thumb is to plan a yearly maintenance budget alongside the build cost rather than assuming the app is finished at launch.

## How can you keep the cost of your first app down?

1. **Build one job well.** List every feature you want, then cut to the smallest set that delivers the core value.
2. **Validate before you build.** Talk to users, build a clickable prototype or test with a simple web version first.
3. **Use a cross-platform approach** when the app does not need deep device features, so one codebase serves iPhone and Android.
4. **Use proven services** for payments, login and messaging instead of building them from scratch.
5. **Plan version two separately.** Ship, learn, then add.

## Do you need an app, or would a website do?

Many businesses that ask for an app need a fast mobile website or a customer portal instead. An app makes sense when people will use it often, need it offline, rely on device features like the camera or notifications, or expect it as part of the product. If your customers visit once a year, a well-built website is usually the better spend. See [how our website builds work](/services/websites/) for that route.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I build an app for a few thousand dollars?

Sometimes, with no-code tools or a very simple app, but a custom app with accounts, a backend and app store releases usually costs considerably more. Be cautious of low quotes that leave out the backend or store submission.

### Is it cheaper to build for iPhone or Android first?

It depends on your users. Many US businesses start with iPhone, but a cross-platform build can cover both for less than two separate native apps. Choose based on where your customers are.

### Who owns the app code?

That should be set out in your contract. A fair agreement transfers the custom work to you once paid and licenses any reusable components, so you can keep running and updating the app.

### What are the ongoing costs of an app?

Expect app store fees, hosting, third-party service usage, yearly operating system updates and support. These continue as long as the app is live.
