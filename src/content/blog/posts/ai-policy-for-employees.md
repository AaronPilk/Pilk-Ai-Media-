Your employees are almost certainly using AI already, whether or not you have said anything about it. A short, clear policy protects customer data, keeps quality up and lets your team use AI confidently instead of quietly. Here is what to include, with a simple template you can adapt.

## Why does a small business need an AI policy?

Without one, people make their own rules:

- Customer details get pasted into free consumer tools.
- AI-written emails go out with wrong prices or invented facts.
- Some staff avoid AI entirely because they are not sure it is allowed.
- Nobody knows which tools the business is paying for.

A policy does not need legal language. It needs to be clear enough that someone can read it in five minutes and know what to do.

## What should an AI policy include?

### 1. Purpose

One or two sentences: the business supports using AI to save time and improve work, as long as customer data stays safe and quality stays high.

### 2. Approved tools

List the AI tools employees may use for work, ideally business plans with clear data protections. Include AI features in software you already use, such as your email, documents and CRM. Say how to request a new tool.

### 3. Information that must never go into AI tools

Be specific:

- Customer personal information such as Social Security numbers, account numbers, financial records and health information.
- Passwords, API keys and login details.
- Confidential contracts, pricing agreements or legal matters, unless the tool is approved for them.
- Anything a client asked you to keep confidential.

### 4. Human review

Anything sent to customers, published publicly or used for a decision must be checked by a person for accuracy, tone and compliance. AI can be confidently wrong about facts, numbers, laws and prices.

### 5. Industry rules

Note rules specific to your business. Real estate: fair housing language and disclosure of altered photos. Mortgage and lending: advertising disclosure rules. Healthcare: patient privacy. Legal: confidentiality. AI-written content is held to the same standards as anything else you publish.

### 6. Disclosure

When to tell customers AI was involved. A common approach: disclose when customers interact directly with an automated assistant, when images are materially AI-generated or altered, and wherever a law or platform requires it.

### 7. Ownership and originality

Do not use AI to copy competitors' content or produce material that imitates real people or brands. Treat AI output as a draft you are responsible for.

### 8. Who to ask

Name a person employees can ask when unsure.

### 9. Review date

AI tools change quickly. Review the policy every three to six months.

## What does a simple AI policy template look like?

> **AI use at [Business Name]**
>
> We encourage using approved AI tools to save time on drafting, summarizing and research.
>
> **Approved tools:** [list]. Ask [name] before using anything else for work.
>
> **Never enter:** customer personal or financial information, passwords, or confidential contracts.
>
> **Always check:** a person reviews anything sent to customers or published before it goes out.
>
> **Industry rules:** [your rules, e.g. fair housing language, advertising disclosures].
>
> **Disclose:** when customers talk to an automated assistant and when images are AI-altered.
>
> **Questions:** ask [name]. This policy is reviewed every [three] months.

This is a starting point, not legal advice. Businesses in regulated industries should have their policy reviewed by an attorney.

## How do you roll out an AI policy?

1. Share it in a short team meeting, not just an email.
2. Show approved tools in action on real tasks.
3. Collect questions and update the policy.
4. Make it easy to find later.

## What are common AI policy mistakes?

The biggest one is writing a policy nobody reads: pages of legal language with no clear examples. Others include banning tools the business already depends on, forgetting AI features built into existing software, never naming who approves new tools, and setting rules once and never revisiting them. Keep it short, give examples of allowed and not-allowed uses, and update it when your tools change.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do small businesses really need an AI policy?

Yes, if anyone on the team uses AI for work. A short policy prevents data leaks and embarrassing mistakes.

### Can employees use free AI tools for work?

For non-sensitive tasks, possibly. For anything involving customer or company information, use approved business plans with clear data protections.

### Should we ban AI at work?

Bans tend to push use underground. Clear rules about approved tools and data are usually safer and more productive.

### How often should an AI policy be updated?

Every three to six months, or whenever you adopt a new tool or a relevant law changes.
